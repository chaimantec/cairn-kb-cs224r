# MDPs and RL notation

The vocabulary CS224R uses to write a decision-making problem down as data, introduced in
[Lecture 1](01-class-intro.md#representing-experience-as-data) and used in every lecture after
it. The notation follows the slides: bold lower-case letters for states, observations and
actions, $t$ for a time step and capital $T$ for the length of a trajectory.

## The pieces

Lecture 1 defines these on slide 32 and recaps them on slides 43–44.

![Slide 32 — How to represent experience as data?](../raw/images/01-class-intro/slide-32.jpg)

*Slide 32 — the pieces of experience as data (state, observation, action, trajectory, reward), with the two running examples: the towel-on-hook robot and a chatbot. [Deck](https://cs224r.stanford.edu/spring_2025/slides/01_cs224r_intro_2025.pdf)*

| Term | Symbol | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| state | $\mathbf{s}_ t$ | the state of the "world" at time $t$ |
| observation | $\mathbf{o}_ t$ | what the agent observes at time $t$; used only when information is missing |
| action | $\mathbf{a}_ t$ | the decision taken at time $t$ |
| trajectory | $\tau$ | the sequence of states (or observations) and actions, $(\mathbf{s}_ 1, \mathbf{a}_ 1, \ldots, \mathbf{s}_ T, \mathbf{a}_ T)$ |
| reward function | $r(\mathbf{s}, \mathbf{a})$ | how good a state and action are |
| initial state distribution | $p(\mathbf{s}_ 1)$ | where trajectories start |
| dynamics | $p(\mathbf{s}_ {t+1} \mid \mathbf{s}_ t, \mathbf{a}_ t)$ | how the world moves to its next state; unknown to the agent |
| policy | $\pi$, or $\pi_\theta$ with parameters $\theta$ | represents behavior, selecting actions from states or observations; see [Policies](policies.md) |

**State.** For a robot hanging a towel on a hook, the state might be the positions of the arm,
the towel and the hook. Without direct access to the towel, it might be an image from the robot's
camera plus the arm's joint positions (Lecture 1, ≈16:57). A typical robot state is the RGB
camera images, the joint positions and the joint velocities (slide 34).

![Slide 34 — Examples](../raw/images/01-class-intro/slide-34.jpg)

*Slide 34 — the two worked examples: the robot's state, action, 200-step trajectory and sparse reward (left); the chatbot's observation, action, conversation and upvote/downvote reward (right). [Deck](https://cs224r.stanford.edu/spring_2025/slides/01_cs224r_intro_2025.pdf)*

**Observation.** An observation may not capture everything needed to make a decision, so past
observations matter too. A chatbot's observation at one time step is the user's most recent
message, and that message alone does not say what the reply should be: the chatbot needs the
conversation's history (≈17:43).

**Action.** For the towel robot, the commanded next joint position; for a chatbot, its next
message, which can be a sentence or a whole paragraph (slide 34, ≈27:00 and ≈28:33).

**Trajectory.** One interaction with the environment. Data in RL is a dataset of trajectories,
not of $(x, y)$ pairs (≈19:14). A trajectory can have length $T = 1$ (slide 32), $T$ need not be
fixed (≈44:42), and a time step need not be a fixed interval: the towel robot runs at 20 Hz, so a
10-second trajectory has $T = 200$, while a conversation advances whenever the user replies
(slide 34, ≈27:48 and ≈29:20). A trajectory generated by running a policy is also called a
*roll-out* or an *episode* (see [Policies](policies.md#using-a-policy-roll-outs-and-episodes)).

**Reward.** Often a function of the state alone, such as whether the towel is on the hook. It
can depend on the action as well, for instance to penalize a walking robot for the torque it
sends to its motors (≈23:54–24:41). Rewards are usually modeled as deterministic; randomness is
placed in the states and actions instead (≈39:18–40:49). See
[The RL objective](rl-objective.md#where-the-randomness-comes-from).

## The Markov property

The next state is purely a function of the current state and action, plus randomness
(slide 33). Given $\mathbf{s}_ 2$ and $\mathbf{a}_ 2$, the next state $\mathbf{s}_ 3$ does not
depend on $\mathbf{s}_ 1$; the dynamics $p(\mathbf{s}_ {t+1} \mid \mathbf{s}_ t, \mathbf{a}_ t)$ are
independent of $\mathbf{s}_ {t-1}$. This independence is the **Markov property**, and it is what
lets RL break a problem up without tracking every earlier dependency (Lecture 1, ≈21:34–22:21).

![Slide 33 — States vs. observations](../raw/images/01-class-intro/slide-33.jpg)

*Slide 33 — the graphical model: states form a chain through the unknown dynamics, each action feeds the next state, and each state emits an observation. [Deck](https://cs224r.stanford.edu/spring_2025/slides/01_cs224r_intro_2025.pdf)*

Observations do not have it. Each observation is a function of the current state and may lose
some of its information, so when the state is hidden, $\mathbf{o}_ 3$ depends on $\mathbf{o}_ 1$
as well as on $\mathbf{o}_ 2$ and $\mathbf{a}_ 2$ (≈22:21–23:07). A tactile sensor is the lecture's
example: touching, losing contact and touching again, the latest reading says little about where
you are while an earlier one says a lot (≈25:28). How much history is needed to recover the state
is heavily domain dependent; current sensor readings are often enough in robotics, much less so
with touch alone (≈23:07–23:54).

The Markov property also constrains how time is handled. With a fixed horizon $T$, a policy that
does not know how many steps remain is not acting on a truly Markovian state; the number of time
steps left has to be incorporated into the state (Lecture 3, ≈36:11–36:57).

## MDP and POMDP

States, actions, a reward function, an initial state distribution and dynamics together form a
**Markov decision process (MDP)**. When the agent has only observations, they form a
**partially observed Markov decision process (POMDP)** (Lecture 1, slide 43, ≈51:39–52:25).

In practice the line between state and observation is a modeling choice. A driving system that
sees only the current LiDAR scan and camera image cannot tell how fast other cars are moving,
but a few seconds of history recovers that, and the history then serves as the state (≈30:07–30:53).
Conversely, a sensor that very occasionally fails is often still treated as giving the state,
because adding a lot of history has real costs (≈32:24–33:12).

## Notation for learning from demonstrations

Lecture 2 adds a few symbols for [imitation learning](imitation-learning.md).

- $\mathcal{D}$ is a dataset of demonstrations, trajectories collected by an expert,
  $\mathcal{D} := \lbrace(\mathbf{s}_ 1, \mathbf{a}_ 1, \ldots, \mathbf{s}_ T)\rbrace$ (Lecture 2, slide 6).
- $\pi_{\text{expert}}$ is the unknown policy the demonstrations are assumed to be sampled from
  (≈3:55).
- $\hat{\mathbf{a}}$ is an action predicted by the learned policy, as opposed to the demonstrated
  $\mathbf{a}$; $\mathbf{a}^{\ast}$ is an action the expert supplies as a correction, and
  $\mathbf{s}'$ a state the learned policy visited (slide 20).
- $\mathbf{a}_ {t,d}$ is dimension $d$ of the action at time $t$, when an action is predicted one
  dimension at a time (slide 10).
- $p_{\text{expert}}(\mathbf{s})$ and $p_\pi(\mathbf{s})$ are the distributions of states visited by
  the expert and by the learned policy $\pi$ (slide 18).

## Notation for policy gradients

Lecture 3 adds symbols for sampled trajectories and their gradients; see
[policy gradients](policy-gradients.md).

- $J(\theta)$ is the RL objective as a function of the policy parameters, and
  $\theta^{\ast} = \arg\max_\theta J(\theta)$ (slide 7).
- $r(\tau)$ is shorthand for the sum of rewards along a trajectory,
  $\sum_{t=1}^{T} r(\mathbf{s}_ t, \mathbf{a}_ t)$ (slide 8).
- $N$ is the number of sampled trajectories, $\tau^i$ the $i$-th, and $\mathbf{s}_ {i,t}$,
  $\mathbf{a}_ {i,t}$ (also written $\mathbf{s}_ t^i$, $\mathbf{a}_ t^i$) its state and action at
  time $t$ (slides 7, 10).
- $t'$ indexes the time steps from $t$ onward in a sum of future rewards (slide 13).
- $b$ is a baseline subtracted from the reward, such as the average reward (slide 16).
- $\alpha$ is the step size in the update $\theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha \nabla_\theta J(\theta)$
  (slide 10).
- $\theta'$ is the latest policy being updated with samples from $\pi_\theta$, and
  $\bar{\pi}$, $\bar{p}(\tau)$ a previous policy and its trajectory distribution (slides 21–22).

## Notation for actor-critic methods

Lecture 4 adds the symbols for value estimation; see [actor-critic methods](actor-critic.md) and
[policy evaluation](policy-evaluation.md).

- $A^\pi(\mathbf{s}, \mathbf{a}) = Q^\pi(\mathbf{s}, \mathbf{a}) - V^\pi(\mathbf{s})$ is the
  advantage, beside the value function $V^\pi$ and Q-function $Q^\pi$ (slide 6).
- A hat marks an estimate: $\hat{V}^\pi_\phi$ is the fitted value network with parameters $\phi$,
  and $\hat{A}^{\pi_\theta}$ the estimated advantage (slides 14, 22).
- $y_{i,t}$ is the regression label for the state $\mathbf{s}_ {i,t}$, and $\mathcal{L}(\phi)$
  the squared-error loss fitted to it (slides 17–18).
- $n$ is the number of observed rewards in an n-step return (slide 20).
- $\gamma \in [0, 1]$ is the discount factor (slide 21), and $\tilde{p}$ the transition
  probabilities of the MDP as discounting changes it.
- $\mathbf{s}'$ is the next state, and $\mathcal{R}$ a replay buffer of past transitions
  $(\mathbf{s}, \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{s}', r)$ (slides 29–31, not discussed in the recording;
  Lecture 5 uses both aloud).
- The deck indexes a time step and trajectory both as $\mathbf{s}_ {i,t}$ and as
  $\mathbf{s}_ {t,i}$ (slide 22 uses the second).

## Notation for off-policy actor-critic

Lecture 5 adds these; see [PPO](ppo.md) and [replay buffers and soft actor-critic](replay-buffers.md).

- $\theta$ is the policy that collected the batch and $\theta'$ the policy after one or more
  gradient steps on it (slide 8).
- $\tilde{J}(\theta')$ is the surrogate objective, "technically not the true objective" $J$, whose
  gradient is the importance-weighted policy gradient (slide 9, ≈27:51).
- $\epsilon$ is PPO's clipping range, about 0.2, so that $\mathrm{clip}(\cdot, 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon)$
  bounds the importance weight (slides 11, 13).
- $\beta$ is the weight on a KL penalty, spoken but not printed (≈26:19), and $\delta$ the bound in
  the KL constraint (slide 10).
- $\hat{A}^\pi_ n$ is the n-step advantage estimate, $w_n$ its weight in the generalized advantage
  estimate $\hat{A}^\pi_ {\mathrm{GAE}}$, with $w_n \propto \lambda^{n-1}$ (slide 12), and $M$ the number
  of gradient steps per PPO update (slide 13).
- $\hat{Q}^\pi_\phi$ is a fitted Q-network. $\bar{\mathbf{a}}'$ or $\mathbf{a}'_ i$ is a next action
  sampled from the current policy rather than taken from the buffer, and $\mathbf{a}^\pi_ i$ an action
  sampled from it at the current state (slides 17–20).
- $p_\theta(\mathbf{s})$ is the current policy's state distribution, which the buffer's states do
  not come from (slide 21).

## Notation for Q-learning

Lecture 6 adds these; see [Q-learning](q-learning.md) and [Bellman equations](bellman-equations.md).

- $\pi^\ast$ is the optimal policy and $Q^{\pi^\ast}$ its Q-function, the one the Bellman
  optimality equation describes (slide 12).
- $\hat{Q}_ \phi$ or $Q_\phi$ is the Q-network with parameters $\phi$; once the policy is greedy
  the superscript $\pi$ is dropped (slides 11–15). $\phi'$ is a frozen copy of $\phi$, the target
  network (slide 18).
- $\phi_A$ and $\phi_B$ are the two networks of double Q-learning (slide 22).
- $\epsilon$ here is the exploration probability of an epsilon-greedy policy, unrelated to PPO's
  clipping range above, and $\vert \mathcal{A} \vert$ the number of actions (slide 14).
- $K$ is the number of gradient steps per round of data collection, and $N$ the number of rounds
  between target-network updates (slides 15, 18). In the n-step target, $N$ is instead the number of
  summed rewards (slides 24–25).
- $y_j$ and $y_{j,t}$ are targets with $j$ indexing the samples in a batch (slides 21, 24).

## Notation for offline RL

Lecture 7 adds these; see [offline RL](offline-rl.md) and
[implicit policy constraint methods](implicit-policy-constraints.md). Its slides 3–11 write
$\mathbb{E}$ and $\mathcal{D}$, and slides 15–28 a plain $E$ and $D$, for the same expectation and
dataset.

- $\mathcal{D}$ or $D$ is the offline dataset of transitions $(\mathbf{s}, \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{s}', r)$,
  and $\pi_\beta$ the unknown **behavior policy** that collected it, possibly a mixture of policies;
  $p_{\pi_\beta}$ is its state distribution (slide 8). $A^{\pi_\beta}$, $\hat{V}^{\pi_\beta}_ \phi$ and
  $\hat{Q}^{\pi_\beta}$ are its advantage and fitted value and Q-functions (slides 17–21).
- $\tilde{D}$ is the filtered dataset of filtered behavior cloning, keeping trajectories whose return
  $r(\tau)$ exceeds the threshold $\eta$ (slide 15).
- $\alpha$ is a temperature in advantage-weighted regression and implicit Q-learning, dividing the
  advantage inside the exponential (slides 19, 23). In conservative Q-learning $\alpha$ instead weights
  the Q-value penalty (slides 25–28).
- $\psi$ is the parameters of the Q-network in slide 20; $\phi$ stays the value network's.
- $\ell_2^\lambda(x)$ is the expectile loss with asymmetry $\lambda$, and $m_\lambda(x)$ an expectile
  curve fitted with it (slide 22); slide 21 writes the loss $\ell_2^\tau(x)$.
- In conservative Q-learning, $\mu$ is the distribution over actions whose Q-values are pushed down,
  $R(\mu)$ a regularizer on it, $\mathcal{H}$ entropy, and $L_{CQL}$ the CQL loss (slides 25–28). Slide 27
  writes the policy's step size as $\eta$, unrelated to filtered behavior cloning's threshold.
